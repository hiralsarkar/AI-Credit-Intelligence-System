A RAROC-Based Policy Frontier for Retail Credit Underwriting

Methodology for the risk-adjusted return on capital framework, the policy frontier, and the constrained selection of an optimal approval cutoff, applied to a retail application portfolio.

Abstract
We formalise a loan-level RAROC framework for retail credit and derive a policy frontier by sweeping the approval threshold over a custom PD model that dominates the incumbent bureau-score process (test AUC 0.756 vs 0.718). Net income, expected credit loss, economic capital, and RAROC are computed per loan from the model outputs. The approval rate is shown to be the solution of a constrained optimisation - maximise net income subject to the approved book's default rate not exceeding the bank's risk appetite - whose optimum is the point where the rising default curve meets the appetite ceiling, at 63.7% approval. The recommended operating point (61.9%) sits one prudential step inside it, beating the incumbent on approval and default simultaneously. We characterise the sensitivity of the result to the loss-given-default assumption and integrate the framework with IFRS 9 staging. All figures derive from 61,503 held-out loans.

Introduction

Retail underwriting conflates two distinct decisions: the model decision (estimating each applicant's probability of default) and the policy decision (choosing which applicants to approve given their risk and return). RAROC provides a theoretically grounded basis for the policy decision by expressing each loan's contribution to shareholder value on a risk-comparable footing. This paper defines the RAROC framework, derives the policy frontier from a dominating PD model, and shows that the optimal approval rate is pinned by the bank's risk appetite rather than chosen by judgement.

The RAROC framework

Definition - loan-level RAROC
For loan i, the risk-adjusted return on capital is net income over the economic capital the loan consumes.
RAROC_i = NI_i / EconCap_i NI_i = Revenue_i - Expected Loss_i - Operating Cost_i = EAD_i × rate - PD_i × LGD × EAD_i - EAD_i × opex EL_i = PD_i × LGD × EAD_i EconCap_i = EAD_i × RW_i × CapitalRatio parameters: rate 12% of EAD, LGD 45%, opex 3% of EAD, capital ratio 10.5%, hurdle 14%

With a flat revenue and operating cost, loan net income reduces to a clean function of PD: NI_i = EAD_i × (0.09 - 0.45 × PD_i), so a loan is value-positive while its PD is below roughly 20%, and RAROC declines monotonically with PD.

Economic capital

Economic capital is assigned by mapping each loan's risk band to a standardised retail risk weight, then applying the capital ratio. Risk weights rise with the band, so higher-risk loans consume proportionally more capital and are penalised in RAROC twice - through higher expected loss and through higher capital.

RW = 0.75 (bands 1-2) | 1.00 (band 3) | 1.50 (bands 4-5) EconCap_i = EAD_i × RW_i × 0.105

Portfolio RAROC

Portfolio RAROC is the capital-weighted average of loan-level RAROC, equivalently total net income over total economic capital. Capital weighting is the correct aggregation: a simple mean overstates portfolio RAROC because low-capital, high-RAROC loans would be over-counted.

Portfolio RAROC = Σ NI_i / Σ EconCap_i

The policy frontier and the optimal cutoff

Sorting loans by PD and sweeping the approval threshold traces a frontier of (approval rate, default rate, net income, RAROC). Net income rises with approval while RAROC and book quality decline; every point below clears the 14% hurdle with wide margin.

The approval rate is not chosen by judgement. It is the solution of a constrained optimisation:

maximise Σ NI_i(q) # net income over the approved set subject to default_rate(q) ≤ d_appetite # d_appetite = incumbent's 3.69% over q ∈ [0, 1]
Result - the constraint pins the optimum

Because net income increases monotonically in q, the constrained maximum lies on the boundary where the approved book's default rate equals the risk-appetite ceiling. Empirically this is q* = 63.7% (default 3.69%, +₹180cr). The same point maximises lending volume and economic profit within appetite, and every approved loan clears the hurdle. The recommended operating point, 61.9%, sits one step inside q* so the approved book's default rate (3.61%) is strictly below the incumbent's - beating the champion on approval and default at once for a ₹4cr income give-up. Below ~62% the policy under-lends; above 63.7% it breaches appetite.

LGD sensitivity

LGD enters both expected loss and economic capital, so the recommended book is tested across the plausible LGD range. Net income stays strongly positive and RAROC well above the hurdle throughout - the policy is robust to recovery uncertainty.

IFRS 9 integration

The RAROC gate selects a high-quality, predominantly low-PD book, which materially improves IFRS 9 outcomes by keeping approved loans in Stage 1. Because true staging requires monthly delinquency data that an origination snapshot lacks, the lifetime ECL machinery - stage allocation, the transition matrix, lifetime PD, LGD from realised losses, and ECL by stage - is built in Module D on a loan-performance panel. Its headline: a Stage 1 to Stage 2 migration raises the provision roughly 39x. ECL improvements are always expressed as a rate (ECL divided by EAD), never as an absolute figure compared across books of different sizes.

Conclusion

The framework converts a PD model into a defensible underwriting policy. Its contributions are an O(N log N) frontier construction from existing model outputs; a constrained-optimisation derivation of the optimal cutoff that ties the approval rate to a single stated parameter (risk appetite) rather than to judgement; and an explicit, auditable link between the cutoff, capital, and IFRS 9 provisioning.

Recommended action

Adopt the custom model and operate at the recommended cutoff (~62% approval), the income-maximising point that strictly beats the incumbent on approval and default. The risk-appetite ceiling is the single policy lever: set the tolerable default rate, and the frontier returns the approval rate that maximises net income at that risk level.
